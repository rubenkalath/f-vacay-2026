33mi

30A End to End

≈600 ft climbing Out & back 2h00–2h20 Zero navigation

County Road 30A runs 18.6 miles from US 98 in the west to US 98 at Inlet Beach in the east, and your driveway sits about two miles in from the west end. Ride to the far end and come back and you have a legitimate morning: fifteen beach towns, a dozen coastal dune lakes, 25–35 mph posted limits, and one genuine climb at Blue Mountain Beach. It is also the only route here where you can put the phone away entirely — you turn left out of the driveway and go straight until the road ends.

The one real decision

The Timpoochee Trail — a paved 10-foot path — parallels 30A for essentially the whole length. Before about 8 a.m. the road is better: smoother, faster, and nearly empty. After 8 the road fills with rental golf carts and beach traffic and the path fills with families, and neither is much good for tempo. Ride the road early and treat the path as a bail-out, not a plan.

Fuel

  • Seaside Central Square

Coast + Two Spurs

≈900 ft climbing Out & back + spurs 2h40–3h00 Best shade on 30A

Route 1 with the two north-running county roads stitched in. CR 283 and CR 395 both climb gently off the coast into the pine flatwoods behind it, and CR 395 through Point Washington — live oaks over the road, Eden Gardens at the top — is the nicest pavement in South Walton that isn't on the water. Neither spur carries much traffic, and both give you ten quiet miles without touching US 98.

90mi

Coast East to Panama City

45 out · 45 back ≈900 ft climbing 6h00–6h30 with stops Turn at 57, 77 or 90

The coast keeps going a lot further than 30A suggests. Past Inlet Beach, Front Beach Road picks the shoreline back up at the Bay County line and runs about twenty miles through Panama City Beach — newly paved, bike lanes both sides, and quiet in September once the summer crowd has gone. At the far end the peninsula does narrow to a point at St. Andrews State Park, but you don't have to follow it there: the Hathaway Bridge carries US 98 over St. Andrews Bay on a protected pedestrian-and-bike lane, and drops you in Panama City proper — a real city, with the St. Andrews marina district and downtown a couple of miles further on. That's your ninety, on the water almost the whole way, and it's the best route here.

Three rides in one

≈57 — turn at Pier Park. Half the PCB strip, all of the coast, back before ten.

The bridge

The Hathaway is about six-tenths of a mile and it's the one piece of this route that needs deliberate handling. It carries a separated pedestrian/bicycle path on the side, protected from traffic — take that, in both directions, rather than the shoulder. It's also the only real climb of the day.

Fuel

    Gas stations, groceries and restaurants for eighteen straight miles. This is the one route with no supply plan — carry two bottles and stop when you feel like it.
  • St. Andrews marina district

78mi

Bay Bridge to Ebro

39 out · 39 back ≈1,300 ft climbing 4h45–5h15 4.5 mi on US 98

The flat way to get long. Over the bay on the Clyde B. Wells Bridge — 2.3 miles of four-lane deck with shoulders, and the only sustained climb on this side of the county — then east on State Road 20, a rural two-lane running through wetland and longleaf between Eglin's boundary and the Choctawhatchee River. Bruce is a crossroads with a store. Ebro is a crossroads with a store, a motel and a restaurant that does home cooking. Almost no traffic, almost no elevation, and nothing to look at but pine — which, if what you want is miles, is exactly the point.

Any distance you want

Turn at the CR 3280 junction near Portland for ≈62, at Bruce Store for ≈70, at Ebro for ≈78. Carry on east past Ebro and you can have a flat 100 — but read the next panel first.

Why this stops at 78

Ebro is the last thing out here. East of it, SR 20 runs twenty-plus miles toward the Choctawhatchee River with no store, no shade and thin phone coverage, and you'd be turning around 50 miles from the house with nothing to fill a bottle from on the way back. Flat, yes. Sensible in September, no.

If you ever want to drop the out-and-back rule, the good big one is a loop: Ebro, then south on SR 79 to Panama City Beach and home along Front Beach Road and 30A. About 95 miles, flat, and supported the whole back half.

Ponce de Leon Springs

51.5 out · 51.5 back ≈2,200 ft · about 21 ft per mile 7h30–8h00 with stops Needs a whole day

The only hundred out of this house with food and water at the far end. Past Freeport, US 331 tilts gradually uphill out of the coastal flats — DeFuniak Springs sits at 259 feet, so it's a 250-foot net drag spread over seventeen miles, not a climb you'd notice on a gradient. Twelve more miles east on US 90 puts you at Ponce de Leon, where a state park spring pushes out fourteen million gallons a day at a constant 68 °F. You turn around by getting in it, then ride home net downhill.

The 79-mile version

Turn around at DeFuniak Springs instead of carrying on and you get ≈79 miles, the same two food stops, and three hours less heat. If the week only has one long morning in it, ride this.

Read this before you commit

At 16 mph plus three real stops this is an eight-hour day. Leave at 6:15 and you're home around two — a working day gone, worth planning around rather than discovering at mile 60.

North of Freeport there's little shade, phone coverage thins, and you're 40 miles out at the far end. Tell someone the plan and turn on live tracking.

The Walton Hill Loop

Loop, not out & back ≈3,000 ft climbing 7h30–8h00 with stops No repeated miles

If you're relaxed about the out-and-back rule, this is the best big ride in the county — Route 5 out, but instead of retracing it you drop south on SR 81 from Ponce de Leon through Redbay and the north Walton ridges to Bruce, pick up SR 20 west back to Freeport, and come home over the bay. A hundred and five miles with essentially no repeated pavement, four supply points, and the only proper rolling terrain within a hundred miles of the house. It still starts and ends at your door — it just doesn't double back.

Ride it this direction

Clockwise, as written. You do the long gradual climb up US 331 in the cool of the morning with fresh legs, and the rolling SR 81 section mid-ride, and you come back across the flat with a net descent when it's hot and you're tired. Reversed, you'd hit the ridges at noon.

The gap to watch

Ponce de Leon to Bruce is nineteen miles with one small grocery at Redbay in the middle. It's the emptiest stretch of any route here — leave DeFuniak with full bottles and don't count on Redbay being open.

Which direction, and why

Two of these go east along the water and three go north into the interior. That's not a preference for climbing — it's what the road network actually allows.

East is the good coastal direction

And it goes further than it first looks. 30A ends at 16.6 miles, Front Beach Road carries you another twenty through Panama City Beach, and the Hathaway Bridge — which has a protected bike and pedestrian lane — takes you over St. Andrews Bay into Panama City at about 45. St. Andrews State Park, at the tip of the peninsula, is a dead-end spur off Thomas Drive, not the end of the road. Turning there gives you 77; carrying on over the bridge gives you 90.

West is genuinely closed

The coast continues toward Destin, but reaching it means about fifteen miles of six-lane US 98 through Sandestin and Miramar Beach — the corridor where most of the region's bike crashes happen, with 45–60 mph traffic, constant turning movements, and debris in the shoulder. The good riding beyond it, out on Santa Rosa Island, is real but isn't worth the price of admission from this address.

North is for variety, not punishment

Inland gets you empty rural two-lane with almost no traffic, and — north of Freeport — the only terrain in the region that isn't table flat. Even the hilliest option here runs about 29 ft of climbing per mile. Go north when you want quiet and a change of scenery; go east when you want the Gulf beside you and a coffee stop every mile.

Is any of this dodgy?

Short version: no, not on these routes at these hours — and the thing most likely to hurt you out here is a car, not a person. Traffic risk is the one worth spending your attention on, and it's why every route above is written to start at first light and why US 98 mileage is minimised. That said, you asked, so here's what the crime data actually says about the places these routes pass through.

Panama City Beach

Per-capita crime figures here are badly distorted: about 18,000 people live in PCB but roughly 375,000 visit every month, so incidents get divided by a resident population a twentieth the size of the actual one. Measured against national averages the city comes out well below them.

What crime there is concentrates hard in spring break — 2025 saw around 770 arrests over a few intense weeks, which is why the city has been tightening the rules. September is the off-season, and Front Beach Road in the morning is families and delivery vans.

Panama City

The city as a whole has a meaningful crime rate — roughly 28 incidents per thousand residents, though still around the national average overall, with property crime the category that runs high. It's not uniform: crime maps put the west side highest, and name St. Andrews and downtown among the safest neighbourhoods in the city.

Which is convenient, because St. Andrews and downtown are exactly where Route 3 turns around. You're there mid-morning, on a marina waterfront, and then you leave.

Inland

Freeport, Bruce, Ebro, Redbay and Ponce de Leon are small rural communities where the realistic hazards are dogs, log trucks and no phone signal. DeFuniak Springs is the one place with numbers worth a glance — it sits in the 81st percentile nationally for violent-crime safety but higher than most Florida towns its size — and you'll be on its main street in daylight, buying a coffee.

What to actually do about it

The one real exposure on a ride like this is your bike while you're not on it, and property crime is the category that runs above average in both Panama City and PCB. Carry a light cable lock, take the bike inside the Publix or keep it in your eyeline at a gas station, and don't leave it on a rack while you sit down to eat.

Beyond that: ride in the morning, be off the road by early afternoon, turn on live tracking so someone knows where you are, and carry a card and some cash. That covers essentially everything on all six routes.

Heat is the limiter, not the terrain

September on this coast averages a high of 87 °F with humidity around 73%, which puts the afternoon heat index well above the air temperature and makes the clock matter more than the profile. Nothing here climbs; everything here cooks. The forecast for your week is unusually kind — the Hurricane Center expects no tropical development anywhere in the Gulf over the next seven days, and the Panhandle is sitting under drier air with isolated 15–25% rain chances — so the plan is simply: start at first light, be done or in shade by eleven.

Water math

Plan on a bottle an hour minimum and closer to a bottle and a half once it's over 85. Two large bottles gets you about two hours, which is why every route above is built around a Publix rather than a nice view.

Take more electrolyte than you think — sweat rates here are high and the salt loss is what ends rides, not the calories. Ice down the jersey at every stop on the century; it buys you a genuine half hour.

Afternoon storms

Even a dry week produces isolated Gulf convection after about noon, and any of it can turn locally strong with 40–60 mph gusts and a lot of lightning. On the flat there is nowhere to hide. Another reason the century starts at 6:30 and not at 9.

Directions, an audiobook, and a phone that survives

What you're describing — a route loaded in advance, spoken turn cues, an audiobook running underneath, and battery left at the end — is exactly what Ride with GPS is built for, and it's the app the touring and club crowd defaults to for this reason. The trick is that you never look at the screen.

Set it up the night before

Build the route on the desktop planner, then in the phone app download Offline Maps for the Walton County area. That's the step that matters: with the maps cached you can ride the whole thing in airplane mode, which kills the single biggest battery drain there is — the radio hunting for signal on SR 20.

Turn-by-turn voice navigation announces each turn out loud and flashes a visual cue over the lock screen, so the phone stays in your pocket, screen off, the entire ride.

The audiobook, legally

Voice cues duck the audiobook automatically on both iOS and Android — the book pauses, the turn is spoken, the book resumes. No conflict.

But Florida Statute 316.304 treats a bicycle as a vehicle and prohibits riding on the road with a headset in both ears; the exception is a single earpiece leaving the other ear open to traffic. One earbud. The restriction doesn't apply on a path separated from the roadway, which covers the Timpoochee Trail but none of the rest of these routes.

Local intel

Nearest shop

Big Daddy's Bike Shop, 2217 W County Hwy 30A — two miles east of the house, directly on Route 1. Full repair, road bikes, and a mobile team that will come to you. Mon–Sat 9–4, Sun 9–2 · (850) 622-1165. Worth knowing before you need a tubeless plug on a Sunday afternoon.

Who else is out there

VELO 30A is the local Strava club and the fastest way to see what people actually rode this week and at what hour. Emerald Coast Cyclists is the organized club, but their weekly rides leave from Fort Walton Beach at 4:45 p.m. Tuesdays and Thursdays — 45 minutes away and at the wrong end of the day for September.

What the locals say about 30A

Consistent advice on the regional forums: riding 30A itself is fine but do it early, because it's a narrow two-lane with heavy tourist traffic and passing a bike requires crossing the centre line. The same crowd is blunt about US 98 — usable shoulders, but 55–65 mph traffic and not somewhere to spend more time than a route requires. Both routes 3 and 4 above are built to spend as few miles on it as the geography allows.
